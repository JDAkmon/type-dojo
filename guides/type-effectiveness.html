Type effectiveness: read the attack, then the defender

Learn the direction of the type chart, the four basic multipliers, and how to remember your first matchups.

A type matchup answers a narrow question: how does the type of an attacking move interact with the target’s defending type? Start by naming those two things in that order. “Water into Fire” describes a Water-type move hitting a Fire-type target. It does not mean that every move used by a Water Pokémon has that advantage.

Four outcomes to recognize

2× — super effective
The type matchup doubles the damage compared with a neutral matchup, all other damage factors held equal.
1× — neutral
The type chart provides neither a bonus nor a reduction. Neutral does not mean weak or useless.
½× — resisted
The target’s type cuts the damage multiplier in half.
0× — immune
The type interaction prevents the attack from damaging the target under the standard rules being practiced.

Start with three pictures

💧 Water puts out fire: Water attacks Fire for 2×. 🔥 Fire burns plants: Fire attacks Grass for 2×. 🌿 Plants absorb water: Grass attacks Water for 2×. These pictures are memory hooks, not scientific explanations of the whole chart.

In this particular triangle, reversing each arrow gives ½×: Fire into Water, Grass into Fire, and Water into Grass. Do not extend that shortcut to every pair. Fighting hits Normal for 2×, but Normal hits Fighting for 1×. The chart is directional, and a weakness does not automatically imply a resistance in reverse.

Work through an attack

  1. Read the move’s type. Suppose the attack is Water.
  2. Read every defending type. For a pure Fire target, there is only one.
  3. Look up Water → Fire: 2×.
  4. Say the relationship out loud: “Water attack, Fire defender, super effective.”

The attacker’s own species does not replace the move’s type in that lookup. A Pokémon may learn attacks of several types. A same-type attack bonus, or STAB, is a separate damage factor; it does not change this chart entry.

A learning routine that sticks

Practice the three forward arrows, then mix in their reverse arrows. Once you can answer without a hint, add a few neutral examples. Returning later is more useful than repeating the same answer immediately until it feels familiar. Type Dojo’s mastery system asks for consecutive unaided answers and a later recall, so recognition has time to become memory.

If you miss an answer, identify the mistake before retrying: did you reverse the attack, read the attacker’s type instead of the move, or forget a defending type? Each error needs a different correction.

Check yourself: Normal attacks Fighting. Is it ½×?

No. It is 1×. Fighting’s advantage against Normal does not create a reverse resistance.
