Generation differences: which type chart should you learn?

Compare Generation I, Generations II–V, and Generation VI onward, with the matchups that changed.

Type Dojo groups games by changes to the standard type-effectiveness chart. The three settings are not claims that every battle mechanic or learnset is identical within a group. They let you practice the type relationships that apply to the game you are playing without switching between generations that share that chart.

Generation I: the original 15 types

Red, Blue, and Yellow use a chart without Dark, Steel, or Fairy. Several relationships differ from later games. Bug and Poison are super effective against each other. Ice attacks Fire for neutral damage. Ghost attacks Psychic for zero damage in the original games, a particularly easy relationship to misremember when you know the modern chart.

Dragon questions need extra care. Dragon Rage, the only Dragon-type move in Generation I, deals fixed damage. A Dragon chart exercise teaches a chart relationship; it should not be read as a prediction that Dragon Rage uses an ordinary type-based damage calculation.

Generations II–V: Dark and Steel

Gold and Silver introduce Dark and Steel. Ghost now hits Psychic for 2×. Poison hits Bug for 1×, Bug hits Poison for ½×, and Ice hits Fire for ½×. In this era, Steel resists both Ghost and Dark attacks.

For example, a Ghost attack against a pure Steel target has a ½× type multiplier here. A player remembering only modern battles might answer neutral. This is why the selected era belongs beside each practice question rather than being an invisible preference.

Generation VI onward: Fairy and revised Steel defenses

X and Y introduce Fairy. Fairy attacks Fighting, Dragon, and Dark super effectively; Poison and Steel attacks are super effective against Fairy. Fairy is immune to Dragon. At the same time, Steel loses its resistances to Ghost and Dark, so those attacks become neutral against pure Steel.

Some species also change typing. Clefairy is Normal before Generation VI and Fairy afterward. Magnemite is Electric in Generation I and Electric/Steel from Generation II. When using a species as an example, selecting a chart era must account for those defending types as well.

Remakes follow their release-era rules

A remake does not automatically use the chart of the game it remakes. FireRed and LeafGreen belong to Generation III, so use the II–V setting. Omega Ruby and Alpha Sapphire belong to Generation VI, so use VI onward. The familiar region is not the deciding factor.

What this setting does not cover

Learnsets, move categories, abilities, and special mechanics can differ within one chart era. The app’s move examples therefore identify an exact species and a named game. Pokémon GO and the trading card game use different systems and are outside this trainer’s scope.

To switch an established habit, practice changed pairs side by side: Ghost → Steel is ½× in II–V but 1× in VI onward. State the era before answering. Your progress is stored separately for each chart era so a correct modern answer does not count as learning the older rule.

Check yourself: which setting fits Pokémon FireRed?

Generations II–V. FireRed is a Generation III remake, even though its setting comes from the original games.
